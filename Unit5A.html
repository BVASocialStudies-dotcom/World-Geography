International Relations

International relations is the study of how countries and other groups interact around the world. Learn these terms, from who holds power to the ways countries cooperate, compete, and connect.

Sovereignty

A country’s full authority to govern itself and control what happens inside its borders. No outside country or group has the right to interfere in its affairs.

Nation-State

A country whose borders closely match a nation, which is a group of people who share a common culture, language, or history. Japan and Iceland are common examples.

Intergovernmental Organizations (IGOs)

Groups made up of member countries that work together on shared goals like trade, security, or health. Examples include the United Nations, NATO, and the European Union.

Non-Governmental Organization (NGO)

A nonprofit group that works independently of any government to address issues like human rights, poverty, or the environment. Examples include Amnesty International and Doctors Without Borders.

Diplomacy

The practice of countries managing their relationships through talks, negotiation, and agreements instead of force. Diplomats and ambassadors represent their countries in these discussions.

Alliances

Formal agreements between countries to support each other, often by promising military help if one member is attacked. NATO is one of the best-known alliances in the world.

Liberalism

A theory that says countries can cooperate and build peace through trade, democracy, and international organizations. It focuses on shared interests rather than competition.

Realism

A theory that says countries act mainly in their own self-interest and compete for power and security. Realists believe power, especially military power, matters most in world affairs.

Proxy Wars

Conflicts in which powerful countries support opposing sides with weapons, money, or training instead of fighting each other directly. Many Cold War conflicts, such as the Soviet-Afghan War, were proxy wars.

Hard Power

Using force or pressure, such as military action or economic sanctions, to make other countries do what you want. It is power based on threats and rewards.

Soft Power

Influencing other countries through attraction, such as culture, values, and ideas, rather than force. Movies, music, and student exchange programs can all be sources of soft power.

Non-State Actors

Groups or individuals that influence world affairs but are not the government of a country. Examples include multinational corporations, NGOs, and terrorist groups.

State Actors

The governments of countries that take part in international relations. They can sign treaties, declare war, and join international organizations.

Globalization

The process of countries becoming more connected through trade, technology, communication, and culture. Because of it, events in one part of the world can quickly affect people everywhere.
